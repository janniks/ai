A language model assigns probabilities to sequences of words. The probability of a whole sequence can always be built up one position at a time: writing $x_1, \dots, x_T${tip:Read this as x one, x two, and so on up to x T. Each x with a little number under it is just a name for one token of the text, in order, so the whole list is the text written as a sequence of numbered slots. The dots in the middle mean the pattern continues for every position in between.} for the $T$ tokens of a text,

$$p(x_1, \dots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$${tip:Read this as the probability of the whole text, from the first token to the last, equals a big product of simpler probabilities. The tall pi symbol means multiply together one term for every position from one to T, and each term is the chance of that position's token given all the tokens that came before it. In plain words, the chance of the whole text is the chance of the first token, times the chance of the second given the first, times the chance of the third given the first two, and so on. This matters because it turns the impossible job of judging a whole text at once into the single repeated job of guessing the next token.}

so modeling language reduces to one repeated question: given the context so far, what comes next? Everything a modern LLM does is trained through this single objective.

But networks consume numbers, not text. Two translations stand between a string and the model: tokenization, which chops text into a vocabulary of discrete units, and embedding, which maps each unit to a learned list of numbers. Both are simple, and both leak into model behavior in ways worth understanding early; many famous failures of LLMs are tokenizer failures.
