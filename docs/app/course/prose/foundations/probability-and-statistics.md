Learning is framed as probability. A model with adjustable parameters $\theta$ assigns each possible output $y$ a probability given the input $x$, written $p_\theta(y \mid x)${tip:Read this as p sub theta of y given x. The p is a probability, the small theta underneath says it comes from a model with those particular adjustable numbers, and the vertical bar is read as the word given. So the whole expression is the probability the model assigns to the output y once it has seen the input x, which is the basic object all of training works on.}. Training adjusts $\theta$ to make the actually observed data as probable as possible; equivalently, it minimizes the sum, over examples, of $-\log p${tip:Read this as the negative of the logarithm of p, where p is the probability the model gave to the correct answer. The logarithm and the minus sign together turn a probability into a penalty score. When the model was confident and right the penalty is near zero, and when it called the truth nearly impossible the penalty becomes huge, so minimizing it pushes the model to put high probability on what actually happens.}. Nearly every loss in this curriculum is this quantity under another name.

Keep three ideas close: conditional probability, how likely something is once you know something else; expectation, the average outcome weighted by probability; and cross-entropy, which scores one probability distribution by how surprised it is, on average, by samples from another. That last one is the loss function of language modeling.
