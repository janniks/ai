A derivative measures how much a quantity changes when one of its inputs is nudged. Collect all of a model's adjustable numbers into one symbol $\theta$ (theta), and write $\mathcal{L}$ for the loss, the single number measuring how wrong the model is. The gradient, written $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}${tip:Read this aloud as the gradient of the loss with respect to the parameters. The upside-down triangle is the gradient symbol, the little theta underneath says which numbers we are nudging, and the curly L is the loss. Together it names one big list holding, for every single parameter, how much the loss would change if that parameter were nudged, which is exactly the information training needs.}, gathers the derivative of the loss with respect to every parameter, and points in the direction that increases the loss fastest. Training is the repeated step $\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \, \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}${tip:Read this as: replace the parameters theta with their old values minus eta times the gradient. The arrow means update and overwrite, and eta is just a small number that controls how big the step is. Since the gradient points uphill toward higher loss, subtracting a small multiple of it moves the model a small step downhill, making it slightly less wrong each time.}, where $\eta$ (eta) is a small number called the learning rate.

The chain rule is the entire mechanism: when functions are applied one after another, the sensitivity of the final output to the first input is the product of each step's own sensitivity. Backpropagation, the subject of chapter one, is nothing more than evaluating this product efficiently, from the loss backward.
