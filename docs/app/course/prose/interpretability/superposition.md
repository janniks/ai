In a space of $d$ dimensions only $d$ directions can be exactly perpendicular to each other, but vastly many can be almost perpendicular, and the toy-models paper shows networks exploit this: when features are sparse, rarely active together, a layer can pack many more of them than it has neurons, accepting small interference in exchange for capacity. The result is polysemanticity, single neurons that fire for unrelated concepts, because concepts live in directions, not units.

The countermeasure is dictionary learning. A sparse autoencoder is trained to rewrite each internal activation as a sum of a few entries drawn from a much larger learned dictionary of directions, and those recovered directions are strikingly more interpretable than neurons. This technique produced the millions of features mapped in Claude.
