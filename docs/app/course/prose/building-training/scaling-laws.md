Kaplan et al. found that test loss falls as a power law: writing $N$ for the number of parameters, the learned numbers inside the model, loss tracks $N^{-\alpha}${tip:N raised to a small negative power alpha, meaning every doubling of model size cuts the loss by the same fixed fraction}, and the same holds for data and compute, smoothly over many orders of magnitude. Chinchilla corrected the allocation: at a fixed compute budget, loss is minimized by growing parameters and training tokens together, roughly twenty tokens of data for every parameter. GPT-3, trained on far fewer tokens than that, was substantially undertrained for its size.

Two readings of these laws coexist. They are an engineering tool, letting labs predict a frontier model's loss from cheap small-scale runs before committing the budget. And they are a scientific puzzle: nothing in the architecture explains why the exponents are what they are, or why capabilities like in-context learning surface at particular scales along the curve.
