The reward model is a network that scores responses, trained on comparisons: shown a prompt, a preferred answer, and a rejected one, its loss pushes the preferred score above the rejected score. The model being aligned is called the policy, and writing $r$ for the reward model's score and $\pi$ for the policy, it is trained, typically with PPO, to maximize

$$\mathbb{E}\left[ r(x, y) \right] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi \,\|\, \pi_\text{ref}\right)$${tip:read this as the average reward score the responses earn, minus beta times the distance between the new model and the original one. The first part says make responses that the reward model scores highly, on average. The second part is a penalty, scaled by the number beta, that grows the further the model being trained drifts from the original pretrained model, which keeps it from gaming the scorer while forgetting how to write well.}

where $x$ is the prompt, $y$ the response, $\mathbb{E}$ means the average over responses, and $\mathrm{KL}${tip:read this as K L, short for Kullback Leibler divergence. It is a standard way of measuring how different two probability distributions are, giving zero when they are identical and a bigger number the more they disagree. Here it measures how far the model being trained has moved away from the original model it started from.} measures how different two distributions are.

The penalty is not a detail. The reward model is an imperfect proxy fit to finite human judgments, and an unconstrained policy will find its blind spots and exploit them, scoring high while producing junk. The penalty tethers the policy to the pretrained reference; the number $\beta$ sets how far alignment is allowed to move the model from what it learned about language.
