LoRA rests on one hypothesis: the change needed to adapt a pretrained model is simple, expressible with far fewer numbers than the model itself. So freeze each big weight matrix $W$, the grid of learned numbers in a layer, and learn only a correction formed by multiplying two thin matrices:

$$W' = W + BA$${tip:read it as: the new weight grid, W-prime, equals the original frozen grid W plus the result of multiplying two much smaller grids called B and A. B and A are skinny, so together they hold only a tiny fraction of the numbers in W, yet their product has exactly the same shape as W and acts as a correction layered on top of it. This is why fine-tuning with LoRA is so cheap: only the small grids B and A are trained while the huge original W never changes.}

where $B$ and $A$ are tall and wide slivers whose product has the full shape of $W$, often well under one percent of the parameters. After training, the product $BA${tip:read as B times A, the two small trained grids multiplied together. The multiplication produces one grid with the same shape as the original weight matrix, which is what lets it be merged back in.} can be folded into $W$, so running the model costs nothing extra. QLoRA pushes further by storing the frozen base at 4-bit precision, which is how large models get fine-tuned on a single GPU.
